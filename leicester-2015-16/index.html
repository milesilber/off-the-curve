Why a run like Leicester City's will never happen again

Leicester won the Premier League in 2015/16. Three things made it possible, and none of them exist anymore.

By Miles Silber

If you have ever found yourself in any aspect of the sports world, chances are you are at least somewhat aware of the Cinderella story that is Leicester City FC winning the Premier League in 2015/16. If you read into it just a little more, you’d see most headlines would explain this impossibility through their 5000-1 preseason odds1 or the fact that they were promoted to the league just prior.2 And, if you follow almost any all-encompassing sports account on any social media platform, the fact they won the league probably became slightly more mind-boggling if you saw a post about how they were now a mid-table team in the third division of English football.3 Those numbers are cool, but today I’m going to show you not only how crazy it was for this team to win the most competitive league in the world, but why a story like this will never happen again in English Football.

It is worth noting that part of this team’s success can, in fact, be accredited to the mishaps of the powerhouse clubs (Chelsea sacking Jose Mourinho mid-season, Manchester City and Arsenal’s inconsistency, and the struggles of Louis Van Gaal’s Manchester United), but that has a chance of randomly occurring again, the three pillars that made the Leicester team specifically able to win the league do not.

The First PillarTheir Playstyle is Now a Baseline, Not a Loophole

Leicester’s playstyle was based on sacrificing possession to ensure defensive solidarity with their low-block defense, and then capitalize off of mistakes with their high-pressing-high-work-rate forwards in order to score on counter-attacks. Their average possession of 42.4% is the lowest of any Premier league champion ever,4 and the possession of every champion since them has been between 55-72%, a minimum of thirteen percentage points higher.5

Well, why? This is because teams figured out that you don’t have to sacrifice possession that can lead to attacking possibilities in order to concede less goals. Just look at Arsenal’s championship side last year, they conceded 9 less goals (27 to Leicester’s 36) and still held 56.4% possession.6

This isn’t to say that this playstyle isn’t somewhat effective, Sunderland and Crystal Palace both had possession hovering around 45%, with the former finishing 7th and the latter winning the Europa Conference League, but neither team came close to the top four, much less winning it all.7

The Second PillarIt Takes Rare Players to Win, Players the Market No Longer Prices Cheaply

How much do you think it would cost to gather four players worthy enough to earn a Team of the Year spot? Maybe around 200 million, 50 each seems fair enough right?

The cost that it took for Leicester City to buy N’Golo Kanté, Riyad Mahrez, Wes Morgan, and Jamie Vardy was not 200 million, not 100 million, not even 50 million, but 10.68 million Euros.89 Less than 3 million Euros each for 4 of the 11 most impactful players in the entire league, and it gets even crazier. The July before their historic season, the average transfer market value of these four was 4.4 million.10 The average transfer value of the PFA Team of the Year selections on championship sides since that season, using the same transfer market value of the July before the season started, has averaged between 44.5 million and 98.2 million per player. That average is 66 million per player, FIFTEEN times more than Leicester’s.11

It’s not like those four suddenly became amazing in a year, the fact is that the modern-day market would NEVER value that sort of talent even remotely close to that low ever again. As a result of the adjustment of the market, it is no longer possible for a small team like Leicester to get their hands on that much talent at one time because they will always be outbid by the bigger club.

The Third PillarThe Financial Door Has Closed

By the Numbers

The average squad value gap between the top 6 Premier League clubs and the rest of the league in 2015/16 was 179 Million Euros, ever since then it has not been below 353 million in any season, peaking at 703 million in 2018/19.13 The gap essentially doubled right after Leicester won the league, and has never been close to that number again. The financial conditions that made Leicester’s title run possible no longer exist.

If we compared the average value of every Premier League champion to the average value of every club that hasn’t won the league between 2015/16 and 2025/26 (Manchester City, Liverpool, Chelsea, and Arsenal), Leicester’s title was 26.4 million BELOW that average, while every other champion since has been above that average by between 452 million and 986 million Euros.14 In terms of market value, Leicester was a below-average Premier League squad, the fact that no champion has been within half a billion of euros of an average squad since then shows that you’ve got to spend big money on high value players to even have a chance at winning the league.

In 7 of the 10 seasons since the historic season, the champion was also the highest-valued squad in the league. Leicester is the only champion who wasn’t even close to the league’s richest club, and by a wider margin (3.58x less than Manchester City’s 576.5 million) than any other champion-to-richest-club gap.15

The wage bill of that Leicester side was 38.45m GBP, the 16th highest of the 20 Premier League sides that year.16 Of the 30 relegated teams since that season, NINE of them still spent more than the season-specific equivalent to that wage bill, including Leicester City itself when they were relegated in the 2022/23 season and the 2024/25 season.17 Furthermore, the best finish for a 16th-ranked-wage club since Leicester is 6th (Bournemouth in 25/26), with two other sides reaching 7th (Wolves in 19/20 and Nottingham Forest in 24/25). The rest of the clubs ranged from mid-table to relegation, with not a single one of them finishing in the top four.18 It’s no longer possible with a small club that can only afford certain wages to even break into the top four, which means they won’t be winning a Premier League any time soon.

The fact is, if you’re not either a big club that has historically always had money, or received a sustained increase in transfer and wage spending due to an ownership takeover like Newcastle and Aston Villa, you can’t even break into the top four. Sure, Leicester had an ownership change six years before their title, but their wages and spending remained low in the years leading up to their historic season, and the takeover itself was primarily reported as debt relief left by the previous ownership.1920 Now more than ever, it is evidently clear that cash is king in the Premier League.
